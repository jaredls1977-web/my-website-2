On Organizational Design

The Myth of Multiple Hats

A woodworking magazine, a machine called the Shopsmith, and a lesson from my father about why doing everything is not the same as doing anything well.

By Jared Scott

Why “One-Size-Fits-All” Usually Fits Nobody Well

There’s a moment at the end of almost every cruise vacation when reality finally catches up with you.

Usually the night before the flight home.

You pull out the nice jeans you wore when you boarded the ship a week earlier, and suddenly those jeans seem personally offended by your existence.

The button is fighting for survival.

That collared shirt now fits like shrink wrap.

And somewhere between the buffets, desserts, midnight pizza, and all the “I’m on vacation” excuses, you quietly accept defeat.

So the next morning, you walk through the airport wearing the universal outfit of surrender: an oversized T-shirt and the most forgiving pair of sweatpants you own.

Comfortable? Absolutely.

Flattering? Not even remotely.

But that’s the thing about “one-size-fits-all.”

It technically works.

It just doesn’t work particularly well.

And businesses do this exact same thing every day.

Somewhere along the way, companies convinced themselves that the ideal employee is somebody who wears multiple hats, fills multiple roles, manages people while also doing production work, solves technical problems, answers customer calls, updates spreadsheets, attends meetings, and somehow still has enough energy left to pretend they’re excited during a Monday morning Teams call.

We praise people for “doing it all.”

Then we wonder why everyone is exhausted, frustrated, average at everything, and exceptional at nothing.

The older I get, the more I realize this isn’t really a people problem.

It’s a design problem.

I learned that lesson long before leadership classes, org charts, or corporate buzzwords.

I learned it sitting in a shed behind our house.

My dad used to build furniture out there.

Not professionally. Just because he loved doing it.

That shed always smelled like sawdust, stain, and motor oil. There were old coffee cans full of screws and nails everywhere, random pieces of oak leaned against the wall, and tools hanging in places that only made sense to him.

In the summertime it was unbearably hot out there, but he’d still spend hours building tables, cabinets, shelves… whatever project had gotten into his head that week.

And I used to sit out there flipping through woodworking magazines while he worked.

I remember coming across this machine called a Shopsmith.

To a kid, it looked incredible.

One machine that did everything. Table saw, drill press, lathe, sander. The ad made it sound like you’d never need another tool again.

And honestly, I thought it seemed smart.

Why buy five tools when one tool supposedly did all of it?

So I showed the picture to my dad and told him maybe someday I’d save up and buy one.

He laughed a little and said: “You’ll never really be happy with that thing.”

I asked him why.

And I still remember his answer because I’ve thought about it a hundred times since then.

“Because it can do everything… but it can’t do anything especially well.”Dan Scott

At the time, I thought he was just being picky because he liked old-school tools and trusted separate machines more.

But the older I get, the more I realize he was accidentally explaining half the problems inside modern companies.

Because businesses love Shopsmith people.

People who can do a little bit of everything. People who wear multiple hats. People answering emails while sitting in meetings about other meetings they probably don’t need to be in.

And on paper, it always sounds efficient.

Until you realize everybody’s tired, managers aren’t really managing anymore, and nobody has enough time left to become exceptional at what they’re naturally good at.

The engineer becomes customer service.

The supervisor becomes a production employee.

The accountant becomes IT support.

And the manager becomes the department firefighter instead of the department leader.

That last one may be the biggest problem of all.

Somewhere along the line, companies started confusing management with participation.

A manager’s job is not to constantly jump in and do everybody else’s work.

A manager’s job is to lead.

To coach people. To develop people. To remove roadblocks. To build accountability. To solve problems before they become disasters.

But when managers spend all day buried in operational work because the company never staffed properly in the first place, leadership disappears.

Then everybody acts surprised when communication breaks down. Why morale drops. Why turnover increases. Why accountability vanishes.

Because nobody’s steering the ship anymore.

The captain is downstairs washing dishes.

Now don’t get me wrong. Flexibility matters.

Especially in smaller businesses, there are seasons where people have to stretch outside their normal responsibilities. That’s reality.

But there’s a massive difference between occasionally helping outside your role and building an entire organization around permanent overload.

That’s not efficiency.

That’s survival mode.

And honestly, I think companies sometimes mistake burnout for commitment.

If somebody’s drowning under six responsibilities, we call them “valuable.”

If somebody’s answering emails at midnight, we call them “dedicated.”

If somebody hasn’t taken a real vacation in years because nobody else knows how to do their job, we call them “irreplaceable.”

That’s not healthy leadership.

That’s organizational dependency disguised as praise.

The truth is, most people thrive when they can focus on what they’re naturally wired to do well.

Not everybody should manage people.

Not every talented employee should become a supervisor.

Not every manager should also be the technical expert.

And not every hardworking employee should wear six hats simply because they’re capable of surviving it.

Capability and optimization are not the same thing.

A Ferrari can technically pull a trailer.

That doesn’t make it a good tow vehicle.

The goal of leadership shouldn’t be squeezing every possible task out of every possible person.

The goal should be putting people in positions where their strengths actually have room to breathe.

Because when people spend all their energy compensating for weaknesses, they rarely have enough left to maximize their strengths.

And eventually, the whole organization starts looking like those oversized airport sweatpants after a cruise vacation.

Loose. Stretched. Comfortable enough to survive…

…but nowhere near performing at its best.
