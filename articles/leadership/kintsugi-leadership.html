Kintsugi Leadership: Embracing Flaws to Build Resilient Teams

The Japanese art of repairing pottery with gold becomes a model for leading people whose cracks are part of the story.

By Jared Scott

In the art of kintsugi (keen-SOO-gee), a centuries-old Japanese craft, broken pottery is repaired with gold, silver, or platinum, turning the fractures into a feature rather than a flaw. The philosophy behind kintsugi—that broken things can become more beautiful and valuable—is a powerful metaphor for leadership. As leaders, we often face the challenge of working with individuals who have weaknesses, imperfections, or past failures. Instead of hiding these flaws, what if we embraced them and worked to create something stronger and more resilient?

The Philosophy of Kintsugi

At its core, kintsugi reflects the Japanese concept of wabi-sabi, which finds beauty in imperfection and impermanence. This philosophy teaches that scars and flaws are not something to be ashamed of but a vital part of an object's—and a person's—story. Leaders who adopt this mindset see the potential in their teams, not despite the cracks but because of them.

Kintsugi shows us that repairing something is not just about fixing; it's about transforming. The gold-filled cracks don't just mend the item; they elevate it, creating a unique and valuable piece. Simon Sinek offers a similar analogy in his talks about leadership, pointing out that if an appliance has a minor issue, we don't discard it entirely. Instead, we repair what's broken because the overall value far outweighs the problem. Leaders must adopt the same approach with their people.

Leadership Lessons from Kintsugi

  1. Recognizing Strengths Amid Weaknesses Just as a kintsugi artist examines broken pieces to understand how they fit together, leaders must identify the good qualities in their team members. A team member may excel in creativity but struggle with time management. Instead of discarding or sidelining them, leaders can focus on nurturing their strengths while addressing their weaknesses.
  2. Repairing with Care Repairing broken pottery with gold is a deliberate and thoughtful process. Similarly, leaders must approach their team's growth with patience and precision. Offering constructive feedback, providing mentorship, or creating a safe space for mistakes can help individuals feel valued and supported.
  3. Creating Something Unique A repaired piece of kintsugi pottery is stronger and more beautiful than it was before it broke. Leaders who invest in their team members' growth often find that their teams emerge more unified, resilient, and innovative. The process of overcoming challenges together builds trust and loyalty.
  4. Celebrating Imperfections A leader who embraces flaws creates an environment where people feel safe to take risks and grow. When individuals are not afraid of their imperfections being exposed, they are more likely to learn, contribute, and thrive.

Practical Applications of Kintsugi Leadership

  • Listen and Learn: Understand the unique challenges and strengths of each team member.
  • Mentor with Intent: Help individuals see their potential and guide them to address their gaps.
  • Celebrate Growth: Acknowledge not only achievements but also the progress made through challenges.
  • Foster Resilience: Encourage a mindset that views setbacks as opportunities for growth.

A Real-World Example

Consider a team member who has faced significant setbacks—perhaps a failed project or a mistake that caused tension in the team. Instead of labeling them as "broken," a kintsugi leader helps them learn from the experience, repairs their confidence with support, and highlights how the lesson contributes to their overall growth. Over time, this team member might become a source of wisdom and strength for others.

Learning More About Kintsugi

To explore kintsugi and its applications to human relationships, I recommend the book Kintsugi Wellness: The Japanese Art of Nourishing Mind, Body, and Spirit by Candice Kumai. While the book primarily focuses on personal wellness, its lessons on embracing imperfections and finding strength in flaws are deeply relevant to leadership and team development.

Conclusion

Kintsugi teaches us that beauty and strength lie in imperfection. Leaders who adopt this philosophy can transform their teams into something greater than the sum of their parts. By valuing the gold within the cracks, we not only repair what is broken but create something extraordinary.

Just as kintsugi artists turn shattered pottery into masterpieces, leaders who embrace imperfection can build resilient, unified, and high-performing teams. As Simon Sinek reminds us, it's not about throwing away what's broken but about having the courage to repair, restore, and elevate it to something greater.
