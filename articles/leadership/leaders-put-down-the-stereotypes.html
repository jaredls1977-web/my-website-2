On Stereotypes

Leaders, Put Down the Stereotypes (and Pick Up the Avocado Toast)

Why writing off a generation, or a person, by a stereotype costs a leader more than it saves.

By Jared Scott

This story begins with a piece of avocado toast. Specifically, "The Millennial Toast," as it's called at The Flying Fox in Greer, South Carolina, one of my favorite coffee spots.

Now, I'll admit something: I'm a 47-year-old Gen Xer. Yes, I know I don't exactly fit the typical avocado-toast-eating demographic—or so the stereotypes would have us believe. We've all heard the jokes: avocado toast for Millennials, IPA-drinking hipsters with man buns, Boomers who "can't technology," and Gen Z glued to TikTok.

It's easy to chuckle at these overgeneralizations, but there's a deeper danger when these stereotypes spill into leadership.

Stereotypes in Leadership

In leadership, stereotyping goes far beyond harmless jokes. It becomes a subtle form of bias that shapes how we view and treat people. We might assume younger employees are entitled or lazy, that older workers can't innovate, or that the loudest person in the room is the most capable leader. These snap judgments not only limit the potential of individuals but also harm the culture and growth of a team.

As leaders, we risk boxing people in—seeing them as caricatures rather than individuals with unique strengths and perspectives. Worse yet, stereotypes can creep into critical decisions: who gets promoted, who gets the best assignments, and who gets left behind.

Danger Zones for Stereotyping

Here are a few ways stereotyping can creep into leadership:

  1. Ageism: Assuming someone is "too young" to lead or "too old" to learn.
  2. Appearance Bias: Judging someone based on their tattoos, clothing style, or hair color.
  3. Cultural Bias: Misinterpreting someone's communication style or work habits due to cultural differences.
  4. Gender Stereotypes: Assigning roles or responsibilities based on outdated ideas about gender.
  5. Educational Assumptions: Overvaluing formal education while undervaluing real-world experience.

Practical Ways to Break the Box

How do we avoid the trap of stereotyping?

  1. Practice Curiosity: Get to know the people on your team. Learn their goals, strengths, and preferences. Ask questions and listen with an open mind.
  2. Challenge Your Assumptions: When you catch yourself making a quick judgment, pause. Ask yourself, "Is this assumption based on fact or bias?"
  3. Celebrate Individuality: Create a culture that values diversity in thoughts, skills, and perspectives. Recognize people for their contributions, not their labels.
  4. Seek Feedback: Encourage team members to call out bias or stereotyping when they see it—yours included.
  5. Lead by Example: Model the behavior you want to see. When others see you valuing people as individuals, they're more likely to follow suit.

A Lesson from Avocado Toast

That morning at The Flying Fox, as I sat with my "Millennial Toast," I thought about how easy it is to fall into the trap of judging based on the surface. But here's the thing: what matters isn't the avocado toast or the stereotype behind it. What matters is the human being sitting in front of you.

As leaders, our job isn't to box people in; it's to help them break free of the boxes they've been placed in by society, or even themselves.

A Powerful Reminder

"Leadership isn't about finding people who fit a mold—it's about breaking the mold to reveal the greatness within each individual."
