The Standard You Walk Past

Every exception teaches the team what the standard really is.

By Jared Scott · Relationships

A high performer gets results and makes everybody around them miserable. They interrupt, belittle new employees, and dismiss questions. The leader knows. The team knows the leader knows. The numbers look good, so nothing happens.

Eventually someone says, “That is just how they are.” What they really mean is: we have decided the behavior is affordable. The people paying for it were not invited to make that decision.

Results have a method

Performance matters. So does how it is achieved. If an experienced employee hits the production target while new people stop asking questions, the team may be trading today’s output for tomorrow’s capability. The damage is not always visible in the metric being celebrated.

Patrick Lencioni’s team model identifies avoidance of accountability as a barrier to effective teamwork.[1] That is a useful challenge for leaders: accountability cannot be reserved for the easy employee or the easily measured result.

Make the conversation specific

“Your attitude needs work” leaves too much room for argument. Instead: “Yesterday, when Sam asked about the setup, you called the question stupid in front of the team. I need questions answered respectfully. What happened from your perspective?”

Listen. There may be workload pressure, missing training, or a disagreement about what happened. Those facts matter. They do not automatically erase the expectation. Explain the required change, agree on support if needed, and set a date to revisit it.

Check your own exception list

Leaders often have an informal list of people who get a pass: the expert nobody can replace, the person with a long history, the one who makes the boss look good. Everyone else notices. If you correct one person for lateness while another routinely changes the schedule without notice, the stated standard becomes a suggestion.

Fairness does not mean every situation gets an identical response. Experience, context, and prior conversations matter. It means differences can be explained by relevant facts rather than favoritism or fear.

Ask yourself: What behavior have I accepted because confronting it would be inconvenient? Name the next conversation, the facts you will use, and when you will follow up. The standard becomes real when it costs you something to uphold it.
