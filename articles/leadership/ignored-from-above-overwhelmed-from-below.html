On Middle Management

Ignored from Above, Overwhelmed from Below: The Reality of Middle Management

The squeeze of managing a team while being managed yourself, and why middle management is harder than it looks from either direction.

By Jared Scott

Leadership in middle management is a balancing act few truly understand. You're close enough to employees to feel their frustrations but far enough from top executives to influence meaningful change—yet still expected to keep everything running smoothly.

Caught between the demands of the workforce and the strategic goals of upper management, middle managers often bear the emotional and operational brunt of leadership. This isn't just a job; it's a minefield of competing expectations, constant pressures, and a unique responsibility to bridge the gap between two vastly different perspectives.

The Invisible Strain on Middle Managers

Middle managers often witness the unfiltered realities of the workforce. Employees confide in them about unfair policies, burnout, and roadblocks to productivity. At the same time, middle managers must interpret and execute directives from executives, many of whom are insulated from these day-to-day struggles.

For instance, imagine a manager in manufacturing whose team struggles with outdated equipment. Employees complain that it slows down production and increases their workload. The manager understands their frustration but also knows that executive leadership has budget constraints and competing priorities. The middle manager must decide whether to push for costly upgrades or try to improve morale by finding creative solutions—while carrying the stress of both outcomes.

This position creates a disconnect: middle managers see and experience parts of the picture that are painfully clear, while lacking the broader strategic view that executives often keep to themselves. But regardless of their limited scope, middle managers can't ignore the symptoms of dysfunction they encounter—burnout, disengagement, and turnover. These signs are real and demand action.

The Disconnect from the Top

Leadership research consistently shows that as executives move higher in an organization, they become increasingly removed from the workforce's daily realities. Known as "distance bias," this disconnect happens because executives focus on strategy, numbers, and long-term goals—often at the expense of human considerations.

For example, consider a retail company that cuts staff hours to save on labor costs during a slow season. The executive team sees this as a strategic financial decision, but the middle managers face the brunt of the fallout—fielding complaints from overworked employees and dealing with dissatisfied customers. To the executives, it's a spreadsheet decision. To the middle manager, it's an everyday crisis.

But let's face it: sometimes the problem isn't just a lack of awareness. There are instances when leaders know exactly what's happening but prioritize profit, growth, or optics over the well-being of their people. This harsh reality leaves middle managers feeling unsupported and employees feeling expendable.

How Middle Managers Can Lead in the Chaos

Middle managers aren't powerless in this equation. Here's how they can step into their leadership potential despite the pressures:

  1. Elevate Employee Voices Use your empathy to collect meaningful stories and data that highlight workforce struggles. Present these issues to executives in terms they care about—such as productivity, retention costs, and profitability.
  2. Take Strategic Risks Don't be afraid to bring uncomfortable truths to the table. Your position gives you access to both employee pain points and operational metrics—use them to paint a compelling picture for change.
  3. Be Honest About Constraints While advocating for employees, be transparent about what's achievable given the organization's goals. Your team will appreciate your honesty even if you can't solve everything.
  4. Build Alliances Partner with other middle managers to present a unified message. There's strength in numbers, and consistent feedback across departments is harder to ignore.

Executives: Are You Really Paying Attention?

To top leaders: your success depends on the people who make your vision a reality. If middle managers and employees are telling you there's a problem, take it seriously.

For example, a CEO in hospitality might only hear filtered reports about hotel operations. But when they visit a property unannounced, they witness firsthand the understaffing issues that employees and managers have been raising. Leaders who take the time to see things for themselves can make more informed and impactful decisions.

Leaders who prioritize short-term wins over long-term employee well-being risk more than just bad morale—they risk the sustainability of their organization. Ignoring the workforce is a costly mistake, one that shows up in disengagement, turnover, and reduced innovation.

Walk the floor. Host open conversations. Show your people you're willing to do more than just listen—you're willing to act.

Hope for Employees and Middle Managers

For employees, don't underestimate the power of sharing your perspectives in constructive ways. Change often starts with one voice. For middle managers, remember that your position matters. While it's not easy to be "in the middle," it's also a place of immense influence. Use it to advocate for the people you lead and to challenge the leaders above you to do better.

Conclusion

Middle managers may feel ignored from above and overwhelmed from below, but they hold the key to bridging the gap. Their ability to lead with empathy and advocate with courage can transform organizational culture in ways that benefit everyone.

As Peter Drucker said, "Management is doing things right; leadership is doing the right things." For middle managers, this means navigating the chaos with authenticity and using your unique perspective to create lasting change.
