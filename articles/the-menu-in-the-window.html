The Menu in the Window

I kept going back to the table where I had once been welcomed.

By Jared Scott
November 25, 2026

The first time I saw the restaurant, I almost walked past it.

It was raining, the kind of rain that finds its way beneath your collar no matter how tightly you pull your coat. I had missed my bus, my shoes were soaked, and I was trying to decide whether I had the energy left to make dinner when I noticed the light in the window. A handwritten menu hung beneath a small blue awning. I stopped to read it, though I wasn’t sure I could afford a meal out. Roast chicken with lemon and herbs. Crisp potatoes. Warm bread. Pear cake with cream.

I was still standing there when the door opened.

“You’ll catch your death out here,” the owner said, smiling. “Come inside.”

I told him I was only looking.

“Then look from somewhere warm.”

He took my coat and hung it near the radiator, led me to a table by the window, and pulled out my chair. A moment later he returned with a small cup of soup.

“I didn’t order this,” I said.

“It’s on the house.”

I wrapped my hands around the cup before I tasted it. The soup was hot and rich, with little pieces of bread floating on top, which was exactly how I liked it, though I had never said so. I ordered the chicken, and he brought extra potatoes because he had noticed how long I’d looked at them on the menu. When I finished, he asked whether I had room for cake.

“I probably shouldn’t.”

He came back with a slice of pear cake and two forks. “I’ll have half,” he said, “so you don’t have to feel guilty.”

He barely touched it. Mostly he stood beside my table and asked me questions: where I worked, whether I had always lived nearby, what I loved to eat as a child. I told him things I hadn’t thought about in years. My grandmother’s kitchen. The yellow bowl she used for mixing. The way she always saved me the crisp corner of anything she baked.

When I left, he held the door and said he hoped I would come back.

I did. The following Friday, and the Friday after that. Soon I was going whenever I had something to celebrate or needed cheering up, which covered most days. The owner remembered the corner piece. If there was a tray of potatoes, he saved the crispiest ones for me. If I came in looking tired, the tea arrived before I asked. Once, after I mentioned that lilies reminded me of my grandmother, a small vase of them appeared on my table.

“You remembered,” I said.

“Of course I remembered.”

He said it as if no other answer were possible.

I told everyone about the place. I took my sister there for her birthday and my friends after work, and I loved watching them discover it, loved the moment their shoulders dropped at the first taste of soup. The owner greeted them warmly, but I felt a quiet pride in knowing what they didn’t: which table caught the last of the evening sun, which cake he would bring if I said I’d had a hard day. Before long it was simply my place. When anyone asked where I wanted to go, I never had to think.

Years passed like that, or perhaps they only seem to now, gathered together around candlelit tables.

I remember the night the chicken disappeared from the menu, because I had looked forward to it all day.

“We’re trying something different,” the owner said. “I think you’ll like it.”

The new dish was all right. The sauce was a little too sweet, but I ate it and thanked him. Menus change, I told myself. That’s what restaurants do.

A few weeks later the pear cake was gone. Then the bread stopped arriving unless I asked for it. The flowers disappeared, and the candles were more often unlit than not. When the owner passed my table he no longer stopped to ask about my day. Sometimes he nodded. Sometimes he seemed not to see me at all. I missed him, even while I sat in his restaurant.

One night I asked whether they might bring back the chicken.

He gave a short laugh. “You know, most people enjoy trying new things.”

“I do, too. I just liked that one.”

“We all liked things that aren’t around anymore.”

I felt foolish then, asking for an old dish as if it mattered, and I looked down and chose something else.

After that, I tried to be easier to serve. I stopped sending back food that arrived cold. If the tea never came, I told myself I hadn’t really wanted it. When the chair by the window broke and was replaced with one that wobbled, I folded a napkin beneath its leg. I learned to be grateful for small things: a fresh roll, a clean glass, the owner pausing at my table long enough to ask, “Everything all right?”

“Yes,” I would say, even when it wasn’t. He was already walking away.

There were evenings when the old place seemed to return. Once, after I had spent most of a meal trying not to cry, the owner came over and sat across from me. He asked what was wrong, and he listened. Before I left, he brought out a slice of pear cake.

I stared at it. “I thought you didn’t make this anymore.”

“We don’t. I made one today.”

It tasted just as I remembered. I walked home lighter than I had felt in months, and I told my sister about it. She said she was glad, but I could hear the caution in her voice, and I didn’t want to hear it. I wanted to keep the evening exactly as it was.

The next week I asked whether there was any cake left.

“No,” he said. “That was a special occasion.”

I waited for him to tell me when he might make it again. Instead he asked if I was ready to order.

I began to notice the new customers.

They were easy to recognize. They came in shaking the rain from their coats, blinking at the warmth, and the owner led them to the best tables. He brought them soup and leaned close to hear their stories. Sometimes he brought cake with two forks. One evening I watched a woman describe a dish her mother used to make, moving her hands to show him the size of the bowl, and I thought of my grandmother’s yellow bowl. I hadn’t thought of it in a long time. I wondered whether the owner still remembered it.

The next time he came to my table, I asked if he might bring me some soup.

“You didn’t order soup.”

“I know. I just saw you bring it to them.”

He followed my glance. “They’re new.”

I waited.

“They’ve never been here before,” he said. “They don’t know what we’re like.”

I looked around the room: at the tables where I had celebrated birthdays and cried over bad news, at the window where I had watched the seasons change, at the coat hooks and the counter and the familiar crack in the plaster above the door. I knew what the restaurant was like. I also knew what it had been.

“I’d still like some,” I said.

He brought it, but set it down so abruptly that soup spilled into the saucer. I thanked him anyway. Then I sat with the cup in front of me, no longer wanting it.

My friends had stopped suggesting other places. For years I had turned them down, telling them I already had somewhere to go, and when they asked about my favorite restaurant now, I said it was fine. If they pressed, I said the owner had a lot on his mind, or the kitchen was short-staffed, or I had probably come on a bad night. I had become very good at explaining the meals I couldn’t finish.

Sometimes, alone at home, I scrolled through old photographs on my phone. There I was at the window table, holding up a slice of cake. There was the vase of lilies. In one picture the owner stood behind my chair with his hand on my shoulder, and I looked so happy that I would study my own face, trying to remember what it had felt like.

Then Friday would come, and I would go back.

One evening I arrived to find my table occupied. The owner had given it to a couple who had never been there before. He saw me at the door but didn’t come over, so I took a seat near the kitchen, where the room was loud and busy. Someone had left a used glass and a crumb-covered plate on the table. I pushed them to the edge and waited.

The owner passed several times, and each time I thought he might stop. At last I caught his attention and asked for a menu.

He set one in front of me. The corners were soft from years of handling, and a stain had spread across the page where the desserts used to be.

“I miss the old menu,” I said.

His expression changed. “We’ve been through this.”

“I know. But I keep coming in hoping—”

“Hoping for what?”

I looked at the empty candleholder between us, a little pool of dust at the bottom.

I wanted to tell him that I had kept the first menu for years. He had let me take it home because I said I loved his handwriting, and I had tucked it into a drawer with photographs and letters, and every so often I took it out to read. I wanted to tell him that I had once felt lucky just to be sitting at his table, and that lately I felt lonelier there than I did eating by myself at home.

But people were waiting behind him, and he was watching me with the tired expression I had learned to dread.

“Never mind,” I said.

I ordered the soup. It came lukewarm, without bread. I ate a few spoonfuls, paid, and put on my coat. The owner was talking to the couple by the window when I left, and he didn’t look up.

Outside, it was raining. I stood beneath the awning and reached into my pocket for my gloves, and one was missing. I knew I should go back in and look for it, but through the window I could see the owner bringing the couple a slice of pear cake.

He set down two forks.

I stayed there until a bus came. Then I stepped out into the rain and caught it.

The next Friday I found the old menu in my drawer.

The paper had yellowed, and the fold down the middle was nearly worn through. I could still make out the chicken, the potatoes, the cake. At the bottom, in the owner’s handwriting, were the words: We hope you’ll come back.

I remembered the night he gave it to me. I had told him how much I loved the way he wrote the descriptions, how even the simplest dishes sounded like something worth waiting for. “Take it,” he’d said. “There’ll always be another one here.” I carried it home carefully, tucked beneath my coat to keep it dry.

Now I sat at my kitchen table with it open in front of me. For years, whenever an evening at the restaurant had disappointed me, I had come home and read it. I would run my finger down the page and remember how good everything had tasted, and I would tell myself to be patient. Perhaps next week the chicken would return. Perhaps the flowers would be back. Perhaps the owner would see me come through the door and remember the soup.

I had never noticed how much of my life I was spending waiting for dinner.

That evening I walked past the restaurant. The blue awning had faded, but the windows still glowed, and through them I could see a candle burning at my old table. For a moment I thought about going in. I imagined the owner looking up, surprised and pleased. I imagined him asking where I had been, pulling out my chair, telling me he had saved the corner piece just for me.

Someone opened the door from inside. A couple stepped out, laughing, and the smell of warm bread drifted onto the pavement.

I stood there until the door closed. Then I kept walking.

A few blocks away I found another restaurant. Its menu was printed on a single sheet in the window. There were no flowers on the tables and no candles that I could see. I read the menu twice before I went in.

A woman greeted me and asked where I would like to sit. I chose a table by the window. She brought water and gave me time to decide. When I asked about the soup, she told me what was in it. She said they were nearly out, but she would check the kitchen.

“I’m sorry,” I said. “You don’t have to go to any trouble.”

She paused with her pencil against her pad. “It’s no trouble to check.”

She came back a minute later. There was enough for one more bowl.

I thanked her twice when she brought it. The soup was hot, with bread on the side. She returned once to ask how it was, and I told her it was good.

I went back the following week. She recognized me and offered me the table by the window. The week after that someone else was sitting there, so she found me another. I ordered tea, and when she brought it, I asked for lemon. The next time I came in, the lemon was already on the saucer.

I looked up at her. “You remembered.”

She glanced down, then back at me. “Would you like another slice?”

I shook my head. She nodded and went to take another table’s order.

I sat there with my hands around the cup. I hadn’t told her about my grandmother’s yellow bowl or the flowers or the cake. I hadn’t explained how long it had been since someone had remembered the lemon. I had simply asked for it once, and now it was there.

My eyes filled before I understood why. I reached for a napkin, embarrassed, though no one was watching. The woman passed on her way to the kitchen, gave me a small smile, and kept going.

On my way home I passed the old restaurant again. People were gathered at the window table, leaning toward one another over their plates, and I could see the owner among them with his hand on the back of a chair. I stopped for a moment. I still missed the pear cake. I still missed the first night, the warm cup in my hands, the feeling that someone had been glad I came in.

Then one of them lifted a glass, and everyone laughed. I smiled too, though they couldn’t see me, and walked home.

I took the old menu from the drawer and laid it on the kitchen table. There was a small grease mark beside the chicken, left by my thumb the night I carried it home. I had been so careful with it, and still I had left a mark. I smoothed the paper flat and read it once more. Then I folded it along its familiar crease and put it back in the drawer.

The next Friday, when it was time for dinner, I left the menu where it was.
