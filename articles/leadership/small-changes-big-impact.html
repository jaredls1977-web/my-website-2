On Listening

Small Changes, Big Impact: What Listening Really Means for Leaders

The difference between hearing your team and actually changing something because of what they said.

By Jared Scott

Early in my career, I worked an entry-level blue-collar job, spending my days in a grease-stained uniform, sweat and grime marking the end of every shift. It wasn't the kind of job where you'd expect to interact directly with corporate leadership, let alone the vice president of the company. Yet, our VP—David—did something extraordinary.

David held regular meetings called "Coffee with David," where employees from all levels of the company came together in a room. Picture this: a mix of people like me in work boots and dirty uniforms, sitting shoulder-to-shoulder with others in khakis and tucked-in button-up shirts. We'd sit in a circle, with David right there among us—not at the head of the table, but as one of us. He'd start every meeting with a simple question:\ "What do you think the company needs?"

These meetings weren't just a formality; they were a forum where everyone's input mattered. It didn't matter if you were on the production line, answering phones, or sitting in the corner office. Your voice had equal weight, and that was no accident. David understood that great ideas—and great leadership moments—can come from anywhere.

One day, during one of these meetings, someone next to me spoke up: "The toilet paper in the bathrooms is terrible. It's like prison toilet paper." The room chuckled, but David didn't laugh it off. Instead, he leaned forward and asked, "What do you suggest we do about it?" The employee replied, "Why don't we upgrade to Charmin—something a little softer?"

By the next week, the company bathrooms were stocked with Charmin toilet paper.

At first glance, it might seem like a trivial change. But that moment left a lasting impression on me. It wasn't just about the toilet paper—it was about what that change symbolized. David didn't just hear what was said; he listened, acted, and showed he cared.

What Leaders Can Learn from David

As leaders, it's easy to focus on the big picture and overlook smaller details that might seem insignificant. But those details often matter deeply to the people you lead. Here are some lessons from David's approach that can make a lasting impact:

  1. Create Opportunities for Open Dialogue\ Leaders need to provide spaces where everyone feels comfortable sharing their thoughts. Whether it's a formal meeting, a suggestion box, or casual conversations, employees need to know their voices matter. It's not enough to open the floor for feedback—you have to create an environment where people genuinely feel heard.
  2. Treat Every Concern with Respect\ What might seem small or insignificant to a leader could feel monumental to an employee. When an employee raises a concern, they're not just speaking up about the issue—they're testing whether you value them enough to take it seriously. Ignoring these concerns risks eroding trust, while addressing them builds loyalty and morale.
  3. Act Quickly When You Can\ Not every suggestion is actionable, but when a change is simple and beneficial, act on it without delay. Quick wins like upgrading the toilet paper can make a significant difference in employee satisfaction. Employees remember leaders who deliver results, even on small matters.
  4. Weigh the Cost of Inaction\ Sometimes the cost of addressing a concern—like spending a few extra pennies on better toilet paper—is minimal compared to the cost of ignoring it. Employees notice when their leaders care about their comfort and well-being. The smallest actions can have an outsized impact on morale and trust.
  5. Communicate Transparency\ Even if a suggestion can't be implemented, explain why. Employees appreciate honesty and effort, even if the outcome isn't what they hoped for. Transparency shows that you value their input, even when you face constraints.

The Big Picture

Leadership isn't just about big initiatives or sweeping reforms. Often, it's the small actions that show your team who you are as a leader. When employees see that you're willing to listen and act, they feel valued, and when people feel valued, they're more likely to engage, trust, and contribute.

David's response to a simple request about toilet paper taught me that leadership isn't about having all the answers. It's about listening, caring, and acting whenever you can. Great leaders aren't afraid to act on the little things because they know those "little things" can make a big difference.

By focusing on small but meaningful actions and fostering an environment of listening and respect, leaders can create a positive and impactful workplace culture that values all contributions.
