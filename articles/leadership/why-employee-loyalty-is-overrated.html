On Loyalty

Why 'Employee Loyalty' Is Overrated

Rethinking the economics and ethics of asking employees for loyalty a company itself doesn't reliably return.

By Jared Scott

In today's workplace, loyalty is often perceived as the ultimate badge of honor. Companies hold long-tenured employees in high regard, believing their dedication reflects a deep-seated commitment to the organization's mission. But as the world around us changes, so do the assumptions that have guided employment practices for decades. Why is it that, despite this loyalty, long-serving employees often find themselves earning less than newly hired counterparts in the same roles? What if the idea of "employee loyalty" itself is outdated—and even holding companies back?

The Hidden Cost of Loyalty: Observations from the Workplace

Many of us have likely observed instances where a new hire starts at a significantly higher salary than an employee who has been with the company for a decade or more. This discrepancy doesn't just create resentment; it creates a financial gap that can devalue the contributions of experienced employees. Imagine a tenured team member—a go-to resource and a steady performer—training a new hire who's earning a higher salary right from the start. This kind of pay disparity is common, and it often leads long-term employees to feel undervalued and overlooked.

The True Economics of Employee Turnover

When companies pay new hires more than their loyal employees, they're reacting to market forces but missing a larger financial picture. Research by the Society for Human Resource Management (SHRM) reveals that replacing an employee can cost as much as six to nine months' salary. Beyond recruiting fees and onboarding expenses, lost productivity, morale dips, and the time invested in training all add up.

If a company instead directed just a fraction of those resources toward regular salary adjustments for loyal employees, they could retain skilled professionals while acknowledging their continued contributions. This approach could save money in the long term by reducing turnover costs and fostering an engaged, satisfied workforce.

Why New Hires Earn More—and Why It's Problematic

The reasons behind these pay disparities are understandable, though flawed. Companies feel pressure to stay competitive in the job market, often resulting in higher starting salaries for new hires. Current employees, however, may find their pay stagnating despite years of increasing expertise. The message becomes clear: loyalty is rewarded with stagnation, while fresh faces receive higher pay based solely on timing.

This mirrors a key theme from George Orwell's Animal Farm, where loyalty and hard work are rewarded only with empty promises. The animals remain dedicated to Napoleon, believing they'll eventually benefit from their sacrifices. Yet, as Orwell illustrates, their loyalty is ultimately exploited, with the powerful taking more while the loyal workers receive less. In the end, "all animals are equal, but some animals are more equal than others," a line that underscores how misplaced loyalty often results in inequality and disillusionment—a phenomenon not far from what long-tenured employees experience today.

The Cost of Disengagement

Loyalty should never be an excuse for undercompensation. A study from Gallup's State of the American Workplace report found that disengaged employees cost U.S. companies between $450 billion to $550 billion annually in lost productivity. Employees who feel undervalued don't just work less; they actively disengage, impacting overall team morale and productivity.

Imagine a workplace where employees feel valued, not only for their years of service but for their daily contributions. These employees are engaged and motivated, not merely because they're loyal, but because they're recognized and compensated fairly. Companies that overlook this connection between fair compensation and engagement are essentially gambling on their productivity and workforce stability.

Shifting the Paradigm: Loyalty vs. Contribution

To foster genuine loyalty, companies must abandon the assumption that length of service alone is a measure of dedication. True loyalty arises when employees feel seen, heard, and respected. Instead of asking for blind allegiance, companies should ask what they can do to keep employees engaged. This shift in perspective doesn't just benefit employees; it also builds a resilient, committed workforce.

One practical solution is to conduct regular market evaluations of salaries and adjust tenured employees' pay accordingly. Another approach could involve creating pathways for ongoing education and growth, allowing loyal employees to feel that their journey within the company has purpose and forward momentum.

As we reconsider the idea of loyalty, we might remember this: "The only loyalty any company should demand from an employee is the loyalty to do their best work each day." Loyalty should not be an entitlement but a dynamic, reciprocal relationship.

Conclusion

It's time to rethink employee loyalty. Instead of treating it as a default expectation, leaders should see it as a byproduct of engaged, respected, and fairly compensated employees. Long-term team members can be invaluable assets, but that value should be acknowledged—not with yearly plaques but with compensation that aligns with their role and contribution.

Rather than asking for loyalty, let's ask what will keep employees engaged, energized, and fairly rewarded. By closing the gap between tenured and new employees, we build a workforce that values staying because they feel valued—a true win-win for everyone involved.
