The Coworker You Keep Misunderstanding

The people beside you are not obstacles to the work. They are part of how the work gets done.

By Jared Scott · Relationships

Imagine two people on the same shift. One wants to move quickly; the other keeps asking questions. By lunch, the first thinks the second is slowing everyone down. The second thinks the first does not care about quality. Neither has asked what the other is trying to protect.

That is how a lot of workplace conflict begins: not with bad character, but with a story we tell ourselves about someone else’s behavior. Sometimes that story is accurate. Often it is a shortcut that saves us the trouble of a conversation.

Stop treating style as motive

Someone who asks questions might be protecting quality, avoiding blame, or trying to understand what changed. Someone who moves fast might be responding to pressure you cannot see. You do not have to approve of the behavior to get curious about its cause.

Try this: “I notice we approach this differently. What are you trying to make sure does not happen?” Then explain your own concern. You may discover two reasonable goals competing for the same ten minutes.

Be useful before you try to be impressive

Patrick Lencioni’s model of an effective team player combines humility, hunger, and people smarts.[1] I like the tension in that combination. Humility without initiative can leave the work for someone else. Drive without awareness can leave a trail of damaged relationships. Being pleasant without accountability can make a team feel good while the problem grows.

On a real team, this can look like sharing what you know, asking for help before a mistake becomes expensive, giving credit in public, and addressing a problem with the person involved before recruiting an audience. None of that requires you to become best friends.

Make the handoff visible

Some of the worst relationship problems are really unclear agreements wearing a human face. “I thought you had it” is not a process. Agree on what will be delivered, when, and what happens if something changes. Then follow through—or say early that you cannot.

Ask yourself this week: What have I labeled a personality problem that might actually be an expectation I never made clear? Start there. A better relationship may begin with a better handoff.

Listen for the work behind the complaint

Consider a new employee who keeps asking the experienced operator for help. The operator is irritated because the questions interrupt production. The new employee is frustrated because the procedure skips steps everyone else seems to know. Both might call the other difficult. Both may be responding to a training gap that belongs to the organization.

Ask what the person needs to do the job well, and be honest about what you need too. The operator might agree to a ten-minute check-in at the start of the shift. The new employee might collect nonurgent questions instead of interrupting every few minutes. When something affects safety or quality, of course, it should be raised immediately. A useful agreement makes room for both the work and the people doing it.

Let people be more than their worst day

A single bad interaction can become a permanent label if nobody challenges it. That does not mean ignoring a repeated pattern. It means checking whether the evidence is a pattern before you treat someone like a problem to manage. Ask yourself what you have actually observed, what you have inferred, and what you still need to ask.

Good coworkers do not have to share a personality, a politics, or a favorite lunch table. They need enough mutual respect to tell the truth, enough reliability to follow through, and enough humility to repair the moments when they do neither.
