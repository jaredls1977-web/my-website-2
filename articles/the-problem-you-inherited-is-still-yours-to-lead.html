The Problem You Inherited Is Still Yours to Lead

You may not have caused the mess. Your people still need to know what happens next.

By Jared Scott · Relationships

You take over a team with an outdated procedure, a schedule nobody trusts, and a veteran employee who has been promised help for two years. You did not make those promises. You did not write the procedure. You did not create the staffing problem.

Congratulations. You are still the leader now.

Do not use history as a hiding place

There is value in understanding how the team got here. There is less value in spending six months explaining why the last leader got it wrong. Every minute spent assigning blame can become a minute your team waits for a decision.

That does not mean pretending you can fix everything. It means telling the truth about what you know, what you do not know, and what you are prepared to do next. Credibility begins with a realistic commitment and grows when you keep it.

Separate the immediate risk from the long repair

Imagine a qualification process that says a person is ready after completing a checklist, even though the checklist does not show whether they can handle the task independently. You may not be able to rebuild the whole system this month. You can identify the highest-risk tasks, ask experienced employees where the gaps are, and agree on how readiness will be checked while the larger process is repaired.

Say who owns each step and when the team will hear back. A temporary control should have a review date; otherwise temporary has a way of becoming the next inherited problem.

Do not promise what belongs to someone else

“I will get you another person” may feel reassuring, but it is a dangerous promise if hiring approval is outside your authority. Try: “I will document the workload, review options with my manager by Friday, and tell you the decision by Monday.” You can own the action and the communication without pretending to own the outcome.

Google’s discussion of team effectiveness includes clarity and dependability among the factors it identified in its own team research.[1] The practical lesson here is simple: people need to know what is expected and whether commitments can be relied on. A leader who communicates a hard no can be more trustworthy than one who repeatedly offers a hopeful maybe.

Start here: List one inherited issue, its current effect, the first decision you can make, and the person who needs an update. You do not have to erase the past to stop extending it.
