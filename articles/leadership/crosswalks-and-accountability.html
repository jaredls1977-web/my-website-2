"Crosswalks and Accountability: Why Leaders Must Own Their Imperfections"

A traffic-stop moment becomes a lesson in owning your mistakes before someone else points them out.

By Jared Scott

This morning, while driving to work, I encountered a moment that challenged my perspective on leadership and accountability. There's a crosswalk at a busy intersection where I often see medical staff jaywalking, darting across the street instead of using the crosswalk. It's a practice I've always found frustrating and unsafe. I've often thought to myself, One day, someone's going to get hit.

Today, however, something different happened. Running late and feeling aggravated, I arrived at the intersection, where a red light held me back—despite the street appearing empty. Then I noticed a medical staff member using the crosswalk, triggering the light change. My initial frustration faded as I realized they were following the very rules I've always wished people would observe.

And yet, I still felt annoyed. Why? It wasn't their action that irritated me. It was my own fault for being late. This moment reminded me of a critical leadership principle: owning our frustrations, taking responsibility for our mistakes, and staying aligned with the values we advocate.

Key Leadership Takeaways

  1. Examine the Source of Frustration\ As leaders, we often feel aggravated by situations that are outside our control. However, it's crucial to take a step back and identify the real source of our emotions. In this case, my annoyance wasn't with the individual following the rules—it was rooted in my own tardiness. Leaders must be willing to evaluate their own behavior before placing blame elsewhere.

"You cannot control the wind, but you can adjust your sails." – Jimmy Dean

  1. Stay Consistent with Your Values\ Policies and rules often exist for a reason, even if they cause occasional inconvenience. Leaders who establish or advocate for guidelines must model consistency and respect for those principles, even when they're personally frustrated. It's hypocritical to demand others follow rules we abandon in moments of impatience.
  2. Take Responsibility for Your Actions\ Leadership isn't about perfection; it's about accountability. Recognizing that my lateness was my fault and not shifting blame to external factors is an example of the humility leaders need. When mistakes happen, leaders should be the first to own them. This builds trust and sets a strong example for others.
  3. Remember the Importance of Perspective\ Situations often appear differently depending on our vantage point. What I saw as a frustrating delay was, in fact, a positive example of someone doing the right thing. Leaders must strive to see situations from multiple angles to make fair and informed decisions.

Practical Applications

  • Evaluate First, React Later: Before reacting emotionally, pause and ask, What's really bothering me here?
  • Own Your Mistakes: When you're wrong, admit it. Apologies and accountability go a long way in building credibility.
  • Encourage Consistency: Praise those who follow policies, even when doing so inconveniences you. Highlight their adherence as an example for others.

Why Leaders Must Reflect on Their Actions

This story serves as a reminder that leadership requires self-awareness. It's easy to project frustration onto others or overlook the root causes of our aggravation. True leaders embrace their imperfections, take responsibility, and strive to grow through moments of self-reflection.

As leaders, we must ask ourselves: Am I holding others accountable for actions I don't hold myself accountable for? The answer to that question can shape the kind of leader you are and the respect you earn.

Who knew that something as simple as a crosswalk could offer so much insight into leadership?
