How to Work With Someone You Cannot Stand

You do not have to like each other to build a workable agreement. You do have to stop handing the conflict the steering wheel.

By Jared Scott · Relationships

Some people are difficult. Some are difficult for you. And sometimes two decent people become terrible at working together because every interaction arrives carrying the last ten.

Maybe they dismissed your idea in front of the team. Maybe you responded in a way that embarrassed them. Now every email sounds hostile and every delay feels deliberate. The work still has to get done, but the relationship has become a second job.

Separate the person from the pattern

Calling somebody an enemy might describe how the relationship feels. It is a poor operating procedure. “She is impossible” gives you nothing specific to address. “We leave meetings without a decision, and the deadline moves” gives you something to work on.

Write down the observable behavior, its effect, and the outcome you need. Then ask for a conversation about the work: “We have left the last two meetings without an owner for the decision. Can we agree who decides and by when?” You are not pretending the history does not matter. You are choosing a starting point that can be tested.

Let them—and then decide what you will do

Mel Robbins’ The Let Them Theory distinguishes other people’s choices from your own response.[1] That is useful here, provided it does not become an excuse to tolerate everything. Let them disagree. Let them have an opinion of you. Then decide whether you need to clarify a commitment, document a decision, set a boundary, or ask for help.

Try: “I’m willing to discuss the disagreement. I’m not willing to continue while we are talking over each other. Let’s take ten minutes and come back.” If the behavior involves threats, harassment, discrimination, or retaliation, use the appropriate organizational reporting channels. A private conversation is not always the right or safest first step.

Do not confuse forgiveness with access

You can stop rehearsing revenge without pretending trust has been rebuilt. Trust grows through a pattern of behavior, not a demand for a clean slate. Make agreements specific, record consequential decisions, and review whether they are kept. A professional relationship can be limited and still be respectful.

Know which battle you are actually fighting

There is a difference between a disagreement about an approach, a competition for limited resources, and a pattern of undermining. They may feel the same in your body, but they call for different responses. A difference of opinion may need a decision process. A resource conflict may need a leader to set priorities. Repeated interference may need documented examples and a formal conversation.

Before the next meeting, write two columns: what happened and what I think it means. “My proposal was interrupted twice” belongs in the first. “They want me to fail” belongs in the second. The second might eventually prove true, but treating it as established fact too early can make you fight an intention you cannot verify.

Use a small agreement as a test

You do not need to repair ten years of history in one conversation. Start with something specific and measurable: “For the next two weeks, can we put changes to the schedule in the shared document by noon?” Decide who does what and when you will review it. If the agreement holds, you have evidence for the next step. If it does not, you have a clearer basis for involving the appropriate manager.

And look at your own contribution without assuming equal blame. Did you leave them out of a decision they needed to know about? Did you respond to their interruption with an insult? Own what is yours. A sincere correction of your behavior does not require you to excuse theirs.

Ask yourself: What action would protect the work and my integrity even if this person never changes? That is where your influence begins.
