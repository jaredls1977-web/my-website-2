Five Minutes Into Camp David

I drove to camp asking God whether He was mad at me. Five minutes after I pulled in, a stranger’s van gave me the perspective I didn’t know I came for.

I began writing this at 3:30 Sunday morning because one of our cabin mates was ready for the day to start much earlier than the rest of us. He had begun flipping the lights on and off, convinced it was time for everybody to get moving. We laughed about it for a good ten minutes, and I never did get back to sleep.

The sun had not come up yet. The crickets were loud outside, and Camp David was quieter than it had been for most of the weekend. I wanted to get some of this down while I was still there because I already knew I didn’t feel the same way I had when I pulled in Friday.

My wife and I recently moved into a new home on some land, something we had looked forward to for a long time. We love it. We finally have the house and property we wanted, and this should be one of those really good seasons of life. In a lot of ways, it is.

It has also been one thing after another since we moved in. Within the first week, one of our family members, the one with a tail, teeth, and lots of fur, decided to remodel the garage door. Apparently, the paint color on the trim was unacceptable, so it was chewed away. I can laugh about that now. I wasn’t laughing at the time.

I’m already on my third mower because the first two had warranty issues. The washing machine did not work. Our propane tank failed, and then we had more trouble with the propane after that. There have been other things too. None of them are life changing, but after a while, I was tired of dealing with all of it.

Every new problem seemed to aggravate me more than the one before it. My dear wife reminded me several times that these things really weren’t that serious and that other people were facing far worse. I knew she was right. I also didn’t particularly enjoy hearing it while I was in the middle of being mad about whatever had just broken.

I had even gotten to the point of asking God what was going on. Are You mad at me? Did I do something wrong? Why does everything have to be so difficult right now?

That is where my head was when I arrived Friday.

• • •

Before I go any further, though, I have to take you back to 2023 because that is where Camp David first entered my life.

That year, I was part of a ReGen group with several other men, and one of them was Skeeter Powell. Skeeter usually had his beloved dog Chief with him. Although ReGen had nothing to do with David’s Table, Skeeter made sure we heard about it just about every week.

David’s Table is a ministry in Greenville that serves adults with different abilities and their families. Skeeter had helped build it, and you could tell very quickly how much it meant to him. He talked about the people, the families, and the relationships that had grown from it. He also told us about Camp David, the overnight weekend camp David’s Table holds each year.

He talked about it a lot.

My friend Keith King had been inviting me too, and he had been doing it for years. This wasn’t some place I suddenly heard about this year. I had known about Camp David for a long time and had always found a reason not to go.

This year, I finally said yes. Part of the reason was that I had grown tired of listening to myself complain.

Because Jared needed some perspective on just how hard Jared thought he had it.

• • •

When Friday came, Keith climbed into the truck with me, and we headed to camp.

We pulled in that afternoon and parked. I went inside, grabbed my T shirt, put on my volunteer badge, and was almost immediately told to stand in line and help unload luggage. There wasn’t much time to wonder what I was supposed to be doing. Campers were arriving, bags needed to come out, and families needed help getting situated.

Then a woman pulled up in a van that had been modified to carry two wheelchairs.

She was in her seventies, and with her were her husband and son. Both men used wheelchairs.

I watched her get out and begin helping them. What I remember most was the tenderness in the way she did it. She was a wife helping her husband and a mother helping her son. She clearly knew what needed to happen and how to do it, and she wanted to handle the things she could herself.

We stepped in, and she graciously accepted the help.

There was no sadness surrounding their arrival and no sense that they were carrying some great burden for the rest of us to notice. They were happy to be there. Her husband and son had joy on their faces, and she was getting her family checked in for a weekend at camp.

Once we had everything unloaded and her husband and son situated, somebody asked me, “Hey, can you drive her van around to the parking area so she can go get her family registered?”

Before I left, she showed me what I needed to do. The seat had been specially modified, and I could see the spaces where the wheelchairs locked into place.

“Here, you have to push this button in order for it to start,” she explained. “It lets you know that the wheelchairs are locked in.”

I pressed the button, started the van, and pulled away. As I drove, I kept thinking about how this was their routine every time they went somewhere. Her husband and son had to be helped into the van, their wheelchairs had to be secured in those spaces, and that button had to confirm that everything was locked before the vehicle would start.

Every time they went anywhere, they went through all of this.

I made it around the corner to the parking lot before the weight of it hit me.

My wife’s words came back immediately. These things aren’t that big of a deal. There are people dealing with much worse problems.

I had heard her say that more than once, and I knew she was right every time. But I had just watched this woman care for her husband and son with so much love, doing what she could and graciously accepting help with the rest. Now I was sitting in her specially modified seat, looking at the spaces where their wheelchairs locked in.

I said, “God, I am so sorry.”

Then I said the part that was probably more accurate.

“I’m sorry I’ve been such an asshole.”

The mower, the washing machine, the propane, and the garage door trim were still frustrating. Sitting in that van, they looked different.

I stayed there for a minute and got myself together. Thankfully, it was bright and sunny outside, which meant I could keep my sunglasses on when I got out and hope nobody noticed the red eyes underneath them.

I parked the van and walked back to camp.

That was about five minutes into my weekend.

• • •

By dinner Friday, I was getting ready to meet the person I would spend most of the weekend with.

At Camp David, volunteers are paired with a camper, your buddy for the weekend.

Mine is a young man who is strong as an ox. Apparently, his high fives are famous around Camp David. Everyone seemed to know that except me, the person assigned to remain within high five range all weekend. The first one landed like a two by four against my palm, and by the second or third, I understood how they had earned their reputation.

He also has a contagious laugh. When he gets going, those around him begin laughing too, and before long, you find yourself joining in even if you’re not entirely sure what started it.

He does not use a lot of words, but that didn’t keep him from wanting to know me. Quite the opposite. He wanted answers. Where was I from? Where were we going? Why was I doing something? Why was I there? He was trying to figure out who this guy was who had suddenly been assigned to spend the weekend with him.

Two words came up more than any others.

Where?Why?

With the words he had, he was getting to know me.

That first night, while we were lying in our bunks, I was treated to an a cappella performance. He started singing his heart out because he wanted to sing. Whether anybody else wanted a concert from the bunk beside them didn’t seem to be much of a concern.

It rained all day Saturday. This wasn’t a little drizzle. It poured.

By then, I had started noticing the freedom people seemed to have at camp. People in wheelchairs were dancing. Someone was signing so another person could follow what was being said. Everyone found a way into whatever was happening. My attention was no longer drawn to what someone could or couldn’t do. I kept noticing how completely they enjoyed what was in front of them.

During club time, my buddy decided I needed to be on the dance floor near the stage. This is important because I have about as much coordination as a newborn baby giraffe.

Normally, I would have been thinking about who was watching and how many phones were pointed in my direction. To be clear, I am fairly certain there is documentation. A couple of my friends were there, so I have no illusions that whatever happened on that dance floor has disappeared into history.

I went down there with him anyway. He danced, and I did whatever it is that I do. We laughed, and after a while, I stopped caring how ridiculous I probably looked.

After lunch Saturday, we climbed into the go karts in the pouring rain.

My buddy and I raced, and I lost. I would like the historical record to show that I remain convinced God smote me with the slowest go kart on the property. I am not saying divine intervention was definitely involved, but I am also not prepared to rule it out.

Unfortunately, I had made a bet with my buddy before the race. If I lost, I had to jump off the high pier into the lake.

I am deathly afraid of heights.

After the race, we went to the lake. The pier looked considerably higher once I was standing on top of it than it had when I agreed to this nonsense. My buddy was not terribly concerned about my sudden psychological crisis. A bet was a bet, so I jumped.

I survived.

That evening, we had western night, and everybody showed up in their western clothes completely committed to it. Nobody appeared embarrassed or acted as though they were too cool to participate.

Then came karaoke, and more than forty people signed up.

That number stayed with me because in most places, especially if you put a group of teenagers together, asking who wants to stand in front of everybody and sing is going to produce some hesitation. Who is watching? How bad am I going to sound? Is somebody recording this?

Here, they simply got up and sang. Some were better than others, but nobody cared. They were having fun.

By the time we went to bed Saturday night, I was having fun in a way I hadn’t in a while.

• • •

That brings me back to 3:30 Sunday morning, sitting in the dark while most of the camp was still asleep. My buddy’s two questions were still in my head.

Where?Why?

He had been asking simple questions because he wanted to know me. I was the one who kept thinking about them after he stopped asking.

Once everyone was awake, we went to breakfast. My buddy still had one more thing to show me before we left. I can now confirm that ketchup does, in fact, go on banana bread. At least according to him, this was already a settled matter. It was new information for me.

After breakfast, we packed our things, loaded the vehicles, and said our goodbyes. Keith and I climbed into my truck and started home.

The problems waiting for me had not gone away. Something else will eventually break because apparently that is one of the privileges of home ownership, and I will probably get aggravated when it happens.

On Friday, I had driven to camp asking God whether He was mad at me because so many things at home had gone wrong. On Sunday, those same problems were waiting for me, but I could no longer look at them as though they were the whole of my life.

Jared had needed some perspective on just how hard Jared thought he had it.

He got it five minutes into Camp David.
