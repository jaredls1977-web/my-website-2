Your Title Is Not a Relationship

People may follow your instructions because they have to. Whether they trust you is a different question.

By Jared Scott · Relationships

A supervisor walks into a meeting and asks, “Does anybody see a problem?” Nobody speaks. The supervisor calls it alignment. Later, the break room has plenty to say.

Silence can mean agreement. It can also mean people have learned that telling the truth is expensive. A leader who cannot tell the difference is making decisions with missing information.

The power in the room belongs to you

When you control schedules, assignments, evaluations, or advancement, your words carry weight whether you intend them to or not. “My door is always open” is not enough if the last person who walked through it was dismissed, punished, or turned into the problem.

Researcher Amy Edmondson describes team psychological safety as a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking.[1] It does not mean every idea is right or every conversation is comfortable. It means someone can raise a concern or admit a mistake without assuming humiliation is the price of honesty.

Trade reassurance for evidence

Ask a specific question: “What part of this plan is most likely to fail on the floor?” Give people time to answer. Thank them for the information. Then show what you did with it, even when the answer is no. If a change is impossible, explain the constraint. People do not need leaders to agree with everything; they need to know that speaking was worth the risk.

There is an equally uncomfortable obligation on the other side: do not hide from accountability in the name of being supportive. An employee deserves a clear standard, timely feedback, a chance to respond, and a fair opportunity to improve. Kindness that refuses to name a problem can leave everyone guessing.

Repair is a leadership skill

When you get it wrong, try: “I interrupted you in the meeting. That made it harder for you to finish your point. I’m sorry. What did I miss?” Do not make the apology a request for reassurance. Listen, correct the effect where you can, and change the behavior.

Listen where the work happens

Ask the person doing the task to show you where the written process and the real process part company. Do not begin by asking why they are not following the document. Begin by understanding what the document assumes, what the equipment actually does, and what the employee has learned to work around.

If the workaround creates risk, address it. If the procedure is wrong, fix it. Either way, close the loop with the people who raised it. A leader who asks for input and never reports back teaches the team that honesty disappears into a hole.

Accountability should have a face and a standard

Suppose an experienced employee routinely belittles newer teammates. Their technical knowledge matters, but so does the damage caused when people stop asking questions. A clear conversation might sound like: “Twice this week, when someone asked for help, you called the question stupid. I need you to answer respectfully, even when you are frustrated. What is getting in the way?”

Listen to the response, make the expectation clear, and follow up. The conversation is not complete when the leader feels relieved for having it. It is complete only when the next steps are understood and the behavior is checked. Apply the same standard regardless of who is most productive or popular.

Trust does not mean that a leader never makes an unpopular decision. It means people can see the reasoning, understand the expectation, and believe the rules do not change depending on who is in the room.

Here is the test: What happens to the next person who brings you unwelcome information? Your answer tells your team more about your leadership than the values on the wall.

Sources & further reading
