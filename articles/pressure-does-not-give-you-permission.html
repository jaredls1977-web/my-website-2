Pressure Does Not Give You Permission

A crisis explains a leader’s behavior. It does not automatically excuse it.

By Jared Scott · Relationships

The line is down. A customer is waiting. Three people are talking at once, and the leader is trying to make a decision with incomplete information. This is when leadership gets stripped down to what is actually practiced.

Some leaders become clearer under pressure. Others become louder. The team learns quickly whether urgency means focus or whether it means somebody is about to be blamed.

Control the next decision, not every person

Start with the immediate question: What must happen to keep people safe and prevent the situation from getting worse? Assign one person to coordinate, identify who has authority to stop or restart the work, and make sure the people doing the task understand the plan. Safety procedures and qualified judgment still apply when the schedule is ugly.

Then distinguish what is known from what is assumed. “We know the machine stopped at 10:15. We do not yet know why. Maintenance is checking the fault; operations is keeping the area clear.” That gives the team a shared picture without inventing certainty.

Calm is a behavior, not a personality

You do not need to sound serene while the building is on fire. You do need to communicate in a way people can use. Give one instruction at a time. Ask the person receiving it to repeat the critical detail. Update the plan when the facts change. If you snap at someone, correct it as soon as the immediate risk is controlled.

Simon Sinek’s Leaders Eat Last frames leadership around caring for the people in your charge.[1] In practice, care during a crisis can look like making sure the person with the expertise is heard, the person carrying out the plan has the resources, and the team is not punished for reporting new information.

When it is over, do not waste it

A review should ask what happened, what the team expected to happen, what helped, and what needs to change. Include the people closest to the work. Avoid turning the conversation into a hunt for the first person whose name can be attached to the problem.

That does not eliminate accountability. It makes accountability more useful by examining decisions, conditions, and actions together. Assign an owner and a date to each improvement, then check whether it actually worked.

The question: After a hard day, are people clearer about the work and more willing to tell you the truth—or are they simply relieved you have stopped talking?
