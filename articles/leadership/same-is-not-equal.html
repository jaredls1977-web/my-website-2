On Global Leadership

Same Is Not Equal: What Global Leadership Keeps Teaching Me

A hard-won lesson from leading across cultures: consistency in values does not mean uniformity in delivery.

By Jared Scott

Some cultures lean toward structure and process. Others lead through relationships. Some prefer directness. Others lead with pause and reflection.

None of those approaches are “better.” But trying to treat them as if they’re all the same? That’s where things break down.

I’ve learned this lesson the hard way.

A few years ago, I was leading a session in a different part of the world. I approached it like I normally would—clear direction, fast pace, outcome-driven.

It wasn’t clicking. The room was respectful, but not engaged.

Afterward, someone pulled me aside and said, “We want to understand who you are before we follow what you’re saying.”

That stuck with me.

It wasn’t that what I was saying was wrong—it just wasn’t landing the way I intended. Since then, I’ve learned to adjust. Same values. Same clarity. But a different way of delivering it, depending on who’s in the room.

• • •

A Full-Circle Moment

This year’s meeting had a moment that caught me off guard—in the best way.

During a conversation with a colleague, I noticed something sitting on their desk that stopped me mid-sentence: a copy of my book, Authentic Leadership: What Really Works.

Thousands of miles from home, and there it was. Not tucked away on a shelf. Not gifted to them five minutes earlier. It was being used.

That was a quiet but powerful moment for me—not because of the ego boost, but because it reflected what this work is really about. Leadership isn’t local. It spreads. It sticks. It shows up in places you don’t expect it to—and hopefully, it helps.

They even asked me to sign it, which was a privilege.

It reminded me that when you lead and write from real experience—not just best practices—you build trust that travels.

• • •

The Problem With Trying to Make Leadership Universal

There’s a push in global companies to define a single model for leadership. One framework. One voice.

And at a certain level, that makes sense—scalability, consistency, alignment.

But here’s the problem: it turns leadership into something that looks good on paper but doesn’t always work in real life.

Trying to create one global leadership style is like trying to furnish every house in the world with the same IKEA layout. It’s clean, it’s efficient, it checks the boxes—but it’s also flat, impersonal, and stripped of character.

Now, to be clear—I like IKEA. I have several pieces of it in my own home. But as a metaphor for leadership across cultures? It holds up.

When we flatten leadership to fit a global template, we lose the very things that make it effective: cultural awareness, emotional intelligence, and the flexibility to meet people where they are.

Leadership doesn’t work just because it’s well-designed. It works when it’s understood, respected, and contextualized for the people on the receiving end of it.

• • •

What This Year Reinforced

I didn’t leave the meeting thinking, “we’re finally all aligned.” I left thinking, “we’re all still learning—and that’s what makes us strong.”

Because leadership that works globally doesn’t come from uniformity. It comes from curiosity, humility, and the willingness to learn from difference instead of resisting it.

• • •

For Leaders Everywhere

If you’re leading across regions, departments, or even generations, ask yourself:

  • Are you leading based on what’s comfortable for you?

  • Or are you paying attention to what actually connects with the people you’re leading?

That difference might be the gap between compliance and commitment.

Same is not equal. And if we’re serious about leading people—not just processes—we need to stop treating it like it is.
