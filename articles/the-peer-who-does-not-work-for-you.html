The Peer Who Does Not Work for You

Influence gets real when nobody in the room has to listen to you.

By Jared Scott · Relationships

There is a special kind of frustration in being responsible for a result that depends on someone you do not supervise. You need their information, their time, or their decision. They have their own priorities, and your deadline may not even make their list.

You can send another email with “just checking in.” You can copy their boss. You can complain that people do not care. Or you can get honest about the relationship and the system that surrounds it.

Equal authority does not mean equal pressure

A peer may look uncooperative because their team is covering an absence, their leader changed the priority, or your request arrived without enough context. They may also be avoiding a commitment. You will not know which from across the building.

Ask: “What is competing with this on your side?” Then ask the harder question: “What am I asking you to give up if you say yes?” A request that acknowledges the cost is more credible than one that pretends there is none.

Build an agreement, not a favor bank

Relationships at work can turn into a private accounting system: I helped you in March, so now you owe me. That ledger may create compliance, but it rarely creates trust. Make the shared outcome explicit instead. What does each person own? What are the constraints? When will you check progress? What will you do if the agreement no longer works?

For example: “I need the specifications by Thursday to keep the training launch on schedule. Is that realistic? If not, what can you deliver by then, and when should we escalate the gap together?” That is more useful than repeatedly asking whether they saw your message.

Disagree without making an enemy

Peer accountability means being willing to name a missed commitment directly. It also means leaving room to learn that your version of the agreement was incomplete. Describe the behavior, explain its effect, invite their perspective, and agree on the next step. If the pattern continues, use the appropriate decision-maker; a relationship cannot replace clear authority.

Lencioni’s framework treats humility and awareness of other people as ingredients of teamwork.[1] Applied to peers, that means being direct about your needs while remaining curious about theirs.

When a relationship runs into the org chart

Imagine training needs a subject-matter expert for a new qualification program, while production needs the same person on the floor. Both managers are right to care about their commitments. Neither can solve the conflict by telling the other to be a better teammate.

Put the tradeoff in front of the people who can make it. “We can release the expert for two hours Tuesday, which delays the production target, or wait until Friday, which moves the training launch. Which consequence are we accepting?” That changes the conversation from whose request is louder to which risk the organization is prepared to own.

Do not save the disagreement for the hallway

Peer relationships can look smooth in meetings and turn poisonous afterward. Someone nods through a decision, then tells their team it will never work. Another person complains to their boss instead of telling the colleague what they need. Soon everyone knows there is a problem except the person who could help solve it.

Raise the concern in the room where the decision is being made. “I can support the goal, but I cannot commit my team to that date without changing another priority.” If you missed that opportunity, go back quickly. Candor is more useful when it arrives before the other person has built a plan around your silence.

Influence does not require winning every disagreement. It requires being clear enough that other people can make informed choices with you—and dependable enough that your yes means something.

Ask yourself: Am I trying to build a shared commitment, or am I trying to make my priority somebody else’s emergency?
