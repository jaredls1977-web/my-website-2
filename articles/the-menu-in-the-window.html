The Menu in the Window

A story about a favorite place, the things we wait for, and what we carry with us when we leave.

Fiction   •   A thoughtful read
A place at the window

The first time I saw the restaurant, I almost walked past it.

It was raining, the kind of rain that finds its way beneath your collar no matter how tightly you pull your coat. I had missed my bus, my shoes were wet, and I was deciding whether I had enough energy left to make dinner when I noticed the light in the window.

There was a handwritten menu beneath a little blue awning. I stopped to read it, though I wasn’t sure I could afford a meal out.

Roast chicken with lemon and herbs. Crisp potatoes. Warm bread. Pear cake with cream.

I was still standing there when the door opened.

“You’ll catch your death out here,” the owner said, smiling. “Come inside.”

I told him I was only looking.

“Then look from somewhere warm.”

He took my coat and hung it near the radiator. He led me to a table by the window, pulled out my chair, and returned with a small cup of soup.

“I didn’t order this,” I said.

“It’s on the house.”

I wrapped my hands around the cup before I tasted it. The soup was hot and rich, and he had put little pieces of bread on top. I hadn’t told him I liked soup that way.

I ordered the chicken. He brought extra potatoes because he’d noticed how long I looked at them on the menu. When I finished, he asked whether I had room for cake.

“I probably shouldn’t,” I said.

He came back with two forks and a slice of pear cake. “I’ll have half, then you don’t have to feel guilty.”

He didn’t eat much of it. Mostly, he stood beside my table and asked me questions. Where did I work? Had I always lived nearby? What kind of food did I love when I was a child? I told him things I hadn’t thought about in years: my grandmother’s kitchen, the yellow bowl she used for mixing, the way she always saved me the crisp corner of anything she baked.

When I left, he held the door open and told me he hoped I’d come back.

I did. The following Friday, and then the Friday after that.

Soon I was going whenever I had a reason to celebrate or needed cheering up, which covered most days. The owner remembered the corner piece. If there was a tray of potatoes, he saved the crispiest ones for me. If I came in looking tired, he brought tea before I asked. Once, after I mentioned that lilies reminded me of my grandmother, a small vase of them appeared on my table.

“You remembered,” I said.

“Of course I remembered.”

He said it as if there were no other possible answer.

I told everyone I knew about the restaurant. I brought my sister there for her birthday and my friends there after work. I loved watching them discover it, loved the moment their shoulders dropped when they tasted the soup. The owner greeted them warmly, but I felt a quiet pride in knowing things they didn’t: which table caught the last of the evening sun, which cake he would bring if I said I’d had a hard day.

Before long, it was my place. When someone asked where I wanted to go, I didn’t need to think.

Years passed that way. Or perhaps the years only seem that way now, gathered together around the candlelit tables.

I remember the evening the chicken disappeared from the menu. I had been looking forward to it all day.

“We’re trying something different,” the owner said. “I think you’ll like it.”

The new dish was all right. The sauce was a little too sweet, but I ate it and thanked him. Menus change, I thought. That’s what restaurants do.

A few weeks later, the pear cake was gone. Then the bread stopped coming to the table unless I asked. The flowers disappeared, and the candles were often left unlit. When the owner passed my table, he no longer stopped to ask about my day. Sometimes he nodded; sometimes he seemed not to see me at all.

I missed him, even while I sat in his restaurant.

One night I asked if they might bring back the chicken.

He gave a short laugh. “You know, most people enjoy trying new things.”

“I do too,” I said. “I just liked that one.”

“We all liked things that aren’t around anymore.”

I felt foolish then, asking for an old dish as if it mattered. I looked down at my menu and chose something else.

After that, I tried to be easier to serve. I stopped sending food back when it arrived cold. If the tea never came, I told myself I hadn’t really wanted it. When the chair by the window broke and was replaced with one that wobbled, I folded a napkin beneath its leg.

I learned to be grateful for small things. A fresh roll. A clean glass. The owner pausing at my table long enough to ask, “Everything all right?”

“Yes,” I would say, even when it wasn’t. He was already walking away.

There were evenings when the old place seemed to return. Once, after I had spent most of a meal trying not to cry, the owner came over and sat across from me. He asked what was wrong. He listened. Before I left, he brought out a slice of pear cake.

I stared at it.

“I thought you didn’t make this anymore.”

“We don’t. I made one today.”

It tasted just as I remembered. On the way home, I felt lighter than I had in months. I told my sister about it, and she said she was glad. I could hear the caution in her voice, but I didn’t want it. I wanted to keep the evening exactly as it was.

The next week, I asked whether there was any cake left.

“No,” he said. “That was a special occasion.”

I waited for him to tell me when he might make it again. Instead, he asked if I was ready to order.

I began to notice the new customers.

They were easy to recognize. They came in shaking rain from their coats, blinking at the warmth. The owner took them to the best tables. He brought them soup and leaned close to hear their stories. Sometimes he brought cake with two forks.

I watched one woman tell him about a dish her mother used to make. He asked her to describe it, and she moved her hands as she talked, trying to show him the size of the bowl.

I remembered my grandmother’s yellow bowl then. I hadn’t thought about it in a long time. I wondered whether the owner still remembered it.

The next time he came to my table, I asked if he might bring me some soup.

“You didn’t order soup.”

“I know. I just saw you bring it to them.”

He followed my glance. “They’re new.”

I waited.

“They’ve never been here before,” he said. “They don’t know what we’re like.”

I looked around the room. At the tables where I had celebrated birthdays and cried over bad news. At the window where I had watched the seasons change. At the coat hooks, the counter, the familiar crack in the plaster above the door.

I knew what the restaurant was like. I also knew what it had been.

“I’d still like some,” I said.

He brought it, but set it down so abruptly that soup spilled into the saucer. I thanked him anyway. Then I sat with the cup in front of me, no longer wanting it.

My friends had stopped suggesting other places. For years I had turned them down, telling them I already had somewhere to go. When they asked about my favorite restaurant now, I told them it was fine. If they pressed, I told them the owner had a lot on his mind, or the kitchen was short-staffed, or I had probably come on a bad night.

I had become very good at explaining the meals I couldn’t finish.

Sometimes, alone at home, I would look at old photographs on my phone. There I was at the window table, holding up a slice of cake. There was the vase of lilies. In one picture, the owner stood behind my chair with his hand on my shoulder. I looked so happy that I would study my own face, trying to remember what I had felt.

Then Friday would come, and I would go back.

One evening I arrived to find my table occupied. The owner had given it to a couple who had never been there before. He saw me at the door but didn’t come over, so I found a seat near the kitchen.

The room was busy and loud. Someone had left a used glass on my table, and there was a crumb-covered plate beside it. I moved both to the edge and waited.

The owner passed several times. Each time, I thought he might stop. At last, I caught his attention and asked for a menu.

He set one down in front of me. Its corners were soft from years of handling. A stain had spread across the page where the desserts used to be.

“I miss the old menu,” I said.

His expression changed. “We’ve been through this.”

“I know. But I keep coming in hoping—”

“Hoping for what?”

I looked at the empty candleholder between us. It held a little pool of dust.

I wanted to tell him that I had kept the first menu for years. He had let me take it home because I said I loved his handwriting. I had tucked it into a drawer with photographs and letters, and every now and then I took it out to read.

I wanted to tell him that I had once felt lucky just to be sitting at his table, and that lately I felt lonelier there than I did eating by myself at home.

But people were waiting behind him, and he was watching me with the tired expression I had learned to dread.

“Never mind,” I said.

I ordered the soup. It arrived lukewarm, without bread. I ate a few spoonfuls, paid, and put on my coat. The owner was talking to the couple by the window when I left. He didn’t look up.

Outside, it was raining. I stood beneath the awning and reached into my pocket for my gloves. One was missing. I knew I should go back in and look for it, but through the window I could see the owner bringing the couple a slice of pear cake.

He set down two forks.

I stayed there until a bus came, then stepped out into the rain and caught it.

The next Friday, I found the old menu in my drawer.

The paper had yellowed, and the fold down the middle was almost worn through. I could still make out the chicken, the potatoes, the cake. At the bottom, in the owner’s handwriting, were the words: We hope you’ll come back.

I remembered the night he gave it to me. I had told him I loved the way he wrote the descriptions, how even the simplest dishes sounded like something worth waiting for.

“Take it,” he’d said. “There’ll always be another one here.”

I carried it home carefully, holding it beneath my coat to keep it dry.

Now I sat at my kitchen table with the menu open in front of me. For years, whenever I’d had a disappointing evening at the restaurant, I had come home and read it. I would run my finger down the page and remember how good everything had tasted. Then I would tell myself to be patient. Perhaps next week the chicken would return. Perhaps the flowers would be back. Perhaps the owner would see me come through the door and remember the soup.

I had never noticed how much of my life I was spending waiting for dinner.

That evening, I walked past the restaurant. The blue awning had faded, but the windows still glowed. Through them, I could see a candle burning at my old table.

For a moment, I thought about going in. I imagined the owner looking up, surprised and pleased. I imagined him asking where I had been, pulling out my chair, telling me he had saved the corner piece just for me.

Someone opened the door from inside. A couple stepped out, laughing, and the smell of warm bread drifted onto the pavement.

I stood there until the door closed. Then I kept walking.

A few blocks away, I found another restaurant. Its menu was printed on a single sheet in the window. There were no flowers on the tables and no candles that I could see. I read the menu twice before I went in.

A woman greeted me and asked where I would like to sit. I chose a table by the window. She brought me water and gave me time to decide.

When I asked about the soup, she told me what was in it. She said they were nearly out, but she would check the kitchen.

“I’m sorry,” I said. “You don’t have to go to any trouble.”

She paused with her pencil against her pad.

“It’s no trouble to check.”

She came back a minute later. There was enough for one more bowl.

I thanked her twice when she brought it. The soup was hot, with bread on the side. She returned once to ask how it was, and I told her it was good.

I went back the following week. She recognized me and offered me the table by the window. The week after that, someone else was sitting there, so she found me another. I ordered tea, and when she brought it, I asked for lemon.

The next time I came in, the lemon was already on the saucer.

I looked up at her. “You remembered.”

She glanced down, then back at me. “Would you like another slice?”

I shook my head. She nodded and went to take another table’s order.

I sat there with my hands around the cup. I hadn’t told her about my grandmother’s yellow bowl or the flowers or the cake. I hadn’t explained how long it had been since someone had remembered the lemon. I had simply asked for it once, and now it was there.

My eyes filled before I understood why.

I reached for a napkin, embarrassed, though no one was watching. The woman passed my table on her way to the kitchen. She gave me a little smile and kept going.

On my way home, I passed the old restaurant again. People were gathered at the window table, leaning toward one another over their plates. I could see the owner among them, his hand resting on the back of a chair.

I stopped for a moment. I still missed the pear cake. I still missed the first night, the warm cup in my hands, the feeling that someone had been glad I came in.

Then one of the people at the table lifted a glass, and everyone laughed. I smiled too, though they couldn’t see me, and walked home.

I took the old menu from the drawer and laid it on the kitchen table. There was a small grease mark beside the chicken, left by my thumb the night I carried it home. I had been so careful with it, and still I had left a mark.

I smoothed the paper flat and read it once more. Then I folded it along its familiar crease and put it back in the drawer.

The next Friday, when it was time for dinner, I left the menu where it was.
