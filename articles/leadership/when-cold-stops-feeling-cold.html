On Complacency

When Cold Stops Feeling Cold: The Danger of Leadership Complacency

A frozen lunch on a sixteen-degree morning is a strange but honest lesson in how quietly a team's, or a leader's, standards can slip.

By Jared Scott

Have you ever experienced something that shifted your perspective in an unexpected way? Yesterday, I had one of those moments while bringing my frozen lunch to work. As I held it in my hand, it felt cold—obviously—but when I stepped outside into the 16°F air (about -9°C for my colleagues across the pond), something interesting happened. My frozen lunch didn’t feel as cold anymore. The extreme temperature outside made it feel relatively warmer in my hand.

If you want a better understanding of this temperature issue, think about coming inside to wash your hands when it’s cold outside. Have you noticed how the cold water from the faucet suddenly feels warm? That “relative warmth” is the same phenomenon I experienced with my frozen lunch.

This scientific concept, rooted in sensory adaptation, is fascinating because it mirrors what often happens in leadership. Over time, we can become so accustomed to certain conditions—like declining performance, lowered standards, or even toxic behaviors—that we no longer recognize them as harmful. Worse yet, we might even convince ourselves that things are fine or, worse, excellent.

• • •

The Leadership “Temperature Shift”

In leadership, this “temperature shift” happens when gradual changes in a workplace environment go unnoticed. A team that was once high-performing might start to struggle. Morale that was once high might fade. Or, worse, subtle toxicity might creep in. Yet, because the change happens slowly, leaders adapt—and assume it’s just the new normal.

This can be particularly dangerous. Over time, we might find ourselves proud of “performing above the bar” without realizing that the bar itself has quietly been lowered. It’s like the water from the faucet: the change isn’t real warmth—it’s just relative.

A quote by Aristotle comes to mind: “We are what we repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act but a habit.” When leaders don’t remain vigilant, standards can erode one small step at a time.

• • •

Spotting the Danger Before It’s Too Late

How can leaders recognize when the “temperature” has shifted? Here are some practical ways to ensure you don’t fall into the complacency trap:

  • Self-Assessment: Step back and assess your own performance and your team’s. Are you truly excelling, or have you redefined success to fit lower standards?

  • Feedback Loops: Actively seek feedback from your team and colleagues. Sometimes, others notice the decline before you do.

  • Compare to Benchmarks: Metrics don’t lie. Compare your team’s current performance against past successes or industry standards.

Much like stepping outside to feel the real temperature, these practices help you recalibrate your understanding of what’s “normal” and what’s truly excellent.

• • •

Zooming Out to See the Bigger Picture

When you’re stuck in the day-to-day grind, it’s easy to lose perspective. But leadership requires stepping back to see the bigger picture.

Think of my frozen lunch. It felt cold in my hand until I stepped outside, where the air was even colder. That contrast revealed the true nature of its temperature. Similarly, stepping back from your work environment can help you recognize when your standards or expectations have slipped.

If you never pause to evaluate, you risk becoming too comfortable with a mediocrity that feels like success.

• • •

Recalibrating Your Leadership Thermometer

If you’ve identified that the bar has quietly lowered, it’s time to recalibrate. Here’s how:

  1. Set New Goals: Reassess and raise the bar for yourself and your team. Push for growth without overloading your team.

  2. Benchmark Excellence: Compare your team’s performance against industry leaders or high performers.

  3. Invite Fresh Perspectives: Seek external feedback from mentors, consultants, or peer groups to challenge your assumptions.

Leaders must consistently evaluate and reset their expectations to avoid complacency.

• • •

A Sobering Reminder

The biggest danger of leadership complacency isn’t just about your personal performance—it’s about your team. If you allow standards to slip, your team will follow suit. Excellence is contagious, but so is mediocrity.

So, whether it’s frozen lunch or cold water from a faucet, let these moments remind us: perceptions can shift, but leaders must stay vigilant. Step back, reassess, and ensure that you’re not mistaking “relative warmth” for actual success.
