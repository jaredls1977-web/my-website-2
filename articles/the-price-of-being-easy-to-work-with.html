The Price of Being Easy to Work With

When keeping the peace requires you to disappear, the peace is costing too much.

By Jared Scott · Relationships

There is a person on almost every team who never says no. They take the extra shift, fix the unfinished work, and say “no problem” when there absolutely is a problem. Everyone calls them dependable. Inside, they are keeping score.

Eventually the resentment leaks out. A sharp comment. A missed deadline. A sudden explosion that surprises everybody except the person who has been carrying it for months.

The uncomfortable question is not only why other people kept asking. It is also why the answer kept being yes when the truth was no.

Nice can become a negotiation nobody knows is happening

A person may hope that being agreeable will earn appreciation, safety, or reciprocity. When that expectation is never stated, the other person may have no idea a bargain exists. Robert Glover’s No More Mr. Nice Guy examines patterns of approval-seeking and concealed expectations.[1] The idea is worth considering without turning a book into a diagnosis: generosity offered with an unspoken invoice can make both people feel cheated.

The answer is not to become harsh. It is to make your choice and your limits visible. “I can help for thirty minutes today, or I can take this on tomorrow. Which would be more useful?” Or: “I can do that, but the current deadline will move. Which priority should change?”

Resentment is information, not a verdict

Resentment may point to an unfair demand. It may also point to an expectation you never voiced. Often both are true. Before assigning blame, ask: What did I agree to? What did I expect in return? Did I say it? What am I willing to offer now?

Robbins’ “let them” and “let me” distinction can help: other people can make requests; you can decide what you will agree to and how you will communicate it.[2] A boundary is not a magic sentence that makes everyone happy. It is a clear statement of what you will do, followed by action you can actually sustain.

Make a repair without writing a confession

If you have been saying yes and building resentment, try: “I agreed to take this on, but I did not communicate the impact on my other work. I need to reset what I can deliver.” Own your part without accepting responsibility for everyone else’s.

The first honest no may feel wrong

If people have come to rely on your automatic yes, a change may surprise them. Some will adjust. Some may be disappointed. That reaction does not automatically mean you made the wrong decision. It may mean the old arrangement worked very well for them.

Start with a pause: “Let me check my commitments and get back to you this afternoon.” A pause gives you time to evaluate the request instead of answering the discomfort. Then give a clear answer and, if useful, a realistic alternative. You do not need a courtroom defense for every limit.

Boundaries depend on context

A supervisor can often decline a request differently than a new employee who depends on a schedule or paycheck. If you do not have the authority to say no outright, ask the person setting priorities to make the tradeoff explicit: “I can take the additional assignment. Which of these two deadlines should move?” Keep a record of decisions where appropriate and seek support if the expectations are consistently unreasonable.

Leaders have a responsibility here too. If the most reliable employee gets every emergency, dependability has become a penalty. Look at who absorbs the work, who receives recognition, and whether the workload can actually be sustained. The answer may require staffing or process changes, not a lesson in personal boundaries.

Generosity becomes more honest when it is chosen. The goal is not to give less in every situation. It is to know what you are giving, why you are giving it, and whether you can keep your word without quietly disappearing in the process.

The question to carry forward: Where am I asking people to respect a limit I have never told them exists? A strong relationship has room for an honest yes—and an honest no.
